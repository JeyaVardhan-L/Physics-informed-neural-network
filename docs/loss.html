05 · The loss
Chapter 05

Putting the law in the loss

Plug the network into the equation. Whatever is left over is the error.

If the network's curve were a true spring, then m x″ + c x′ + k x would be zero at every time. It won't be, and what is left is called the residual. Square it, average it over some times, and it is a loss like any other.

L = mean10 dots (x̂(ti) − xi)² + λ · mean60 times r(tj)²
r = (m x̂″ + c x̂′ + k x̂) / k

The 60 times are called collocation points. They are evenly spaced over the whole second and carry no measurements. Nobody needs to know the position there, because the residual is a statement about the network alone. Adding more of them costs computing time and no experiments.

Neither term is enough

The equation alone has infinitely many solutions, one for each starting position and velocity. One of them is x = 0, which is also the easiest curve for a fresh network to produce. A test records that its physics loss is exactly zero.

The dots alone have infinitely many continuations, as chapter 1 showed. Together they leave one. The equation narrows every possible curve to a family with two unknowns, and ten dots are more than enough for two unknowns.

The network is never told the starting position or velocity. They are implied by the dots. The true curve never appears in the loss either: only m, c and k are passed in.

Why divide by k

With x near 1 and k = 225, the raw residual is in newtons and reaches the hundreds, so its square reaches tens of thousands. The data term is a squared position, below 1. Added together, the dots would be invisible. Dividing by k turns a force into the equivalent displacement, so both terms start with the same units and a similar size. This is nondimensionalisation in its smallest form, and it is worth doing before touching λ. the line

Where the equation is enforced

The residual is checked only at the collocation points. If those stop at 0.5 seconds, so does the physics.

With the equation only where the dots are, the error afterwards is 1.1. With the same 60 points spread over the full second it is 3.6 × 10−6. This also limits the method: a PINN predicts as far as its collocation points reach. Asking this one about t = 2 would be extrapolation again, with all the trouble of chapter 1.

How loud is the equation?

λ sets the exchange rate between the two terms. The noise in the dots has a variance of 10−4, so a fit that is honest about noise should end with a data loss near 10−4. Much lower means the curve is following noise.

The value used everywhere, λ = 10, was the best of 0.1, 1 and 10 on seeds 100 to 102, and the reported results use seeds 0 to 9. Choosing a setting on the same runs that are reported would make the results look better than they are.

This sweep was run later, and λ = 1000 does slightly better than 10: 2.8 × 10−6 against 3.6 × 10−6. The setting stays at 10, because changing it now would mean tuning on the reported seeds. The direction makes sense. The equation is exactly true here, so there is no cost in trusting it more.

There is a statistical reading. If the noise is Gaussian with variance σ², the data term weighted by 1/σ² is a log-likelihood, and the physics term acts as a log-prior whose strength says how much the equation is trusted. Here the equation is exactly true, so a strong prior is right. If it were only approximately true, a large λ would force the curve onto a wrong law, and chapter 8 measures that.

IN:How do you balance the data loss and the physics loss?
OUT:

First make the units comparable, here by dividing the residual by k. Then sweep the weight over powers of ten on seeds kept apart for tuning. Then sanity check: the data loss should settle near the noise variance. Far below it, the model is fitting noise and the physics is too quiet. Far above it, the physics is drowning the measurements or the equation is wrong. There are adaptive schemes that rescale the terms by their gradient sizes during training. This problem is small enough that a fixed weight works.
