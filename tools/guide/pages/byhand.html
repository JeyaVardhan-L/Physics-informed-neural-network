title: Every gradient by hand short: By hand hint: no autograd lede: The same network in NumPy, with the derivatives of the derivatives worked out on paper. ---

Autograd is convenient enough to hide what it does. This chapter removes it. pinn/scratch.py trains the same kind of PINN with NumPy alone, and its gradients agree with PyTorch's to nine digits for every weight.

Forward: carry three numbers

Instead of passing a value a from layer to layer, pass (a, a′, a″): the value, its first time derivative and its second. Chapter 4 stepped through this with one neuron. In general there are two rules.

A linear layer z = W a + b has constant coefficients, so it acts on each of the three in the same way. The bias is a constant and drops out of both derivatives:

z = W a + b,   z′ = W a′,   z″ = W a″

The activation h = σ(z) needs the chain rule once, and then the chain and product rules together:

h′ = σ′(z) z′,    h″ = σ″(z) z′² + σ′(z) z″

The first term of h″ is the bend of the activation. The second is the bend that z brought with it. This is forward-mode differentiation to second order, also called Taylor mode. The cost is three passes' worth of multiplications, whatever the number of weights.

Backward: share the blame

Now the loss depends on x, x′ and x″. Write g, g1 and g2 for the derivative of the loss with respect to a layer's three outputs. For the residual r = (m x″ + c x′ + k x)/k and the loss λ·mean(r²) over N points, they start as

g = 2λr/N,    g1 = (c/k) g,    g2 = (m/k) g

To go back through the activation, ask where each of z, z′ and z″ was used. z″ appears only in h″. z′ appears in h′ and in h″, squared. z appears in all three, inside σ, σ′ and σ″:

gz″ = g2 σ′
gz′ = g1 σ′ + 2 g2 σ″ z′
gz = g σ′ + g1 σ″ z′ + g2 (σ‴ z′² + σ″ z″)

The third derivative of tanh turns up in the last line. Training a second-order ODE needs σ‴, which is why activations that are smooth to only second order are a poor fit. For tanh, with h = tanh z:

σ′ = 1 − h²,   σ″ = −2hσ′,   σ‴ = −2σ′(1 − 3h²)

Through the linear layer, the weight matrix was used three times, so its gradient is a sum of three outer products. The bias was used once:

dW = gz aT + gz′ a′T + gz″ a″T,    db = gz

That is the whole algorithm. In code it is six lines inside a loop.

Checking it

A wrong gradient rarely crashes. The network trains a little worse and nothing says why. So the derivation is checked three ways. Every weight's gradient is compared with autograd. The JavaScript port is compared with NumPy. And the figure below compares it with finite differences while you watch.

The valley is the argument from chapter 4 in one picture. Even at its best step size, a central difference agrees with the exact gradient to only about nine or ten digits, and it costs two full loss evaluations for each weight. The hand-written pass gives all of them at once for roughly the price of one evaluation and its reverse.

IN:How would you compute PINN gradients without autograd?
OUT:

Propagate value, first and second time derivative forward through the layers together. Linear layers apply the same matrix to all three and the activation applies the chain rule. Then do a reverse pass with three adjoints, one for each. Each weight matrix was used three times, so its gradient is a sum of three outer products. The reverse pass through a tanh needs its third derivative. I would verify it against autograd and against central differences.
