Ten dots and a law

A weight hangs on a spring. It is measured ten times in its first 0.3 seconds, with a little noise. Where is it for the next 0.7?

A neural network trained on the ten dots fits them well and then guesses. A second network, with the same starting weights, the same dots and the same size, is also required to satisfy one line of physics, m x″ + c x′ + k x = 0, at times where nobody measured anything. Its curve stays on the true motion for the whole second.

The second network is a physics-informed neural network, or PINN. This guide builds one in PyTorch, then builds it again in NumPy with every gradient derived by hand, then runs that version in your browser. It measures what each part does by removing it.

It also covers where the idea fails, when a plain least-squares fit does the same job in microseconds, and why a tiny training loss can belong to a completely wrong answer.
